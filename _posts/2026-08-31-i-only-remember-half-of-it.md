By the second week of September, the new timetable is stuck to the fridge, and you might be the only one in the house who has read it properly. Swimming has moved to Wednesday. The childminder does Thursday pickups now, or possibly Tuesday. There is a new WhatsApp group, a new class teacher, and a reading record with a different coloured cover. Someone says the words "fresh start", and you nod, and think: not this week.

For most of the family, September really is a reset. New class, clean pencil case. For the person who holds the routine it runs the other way. It is a term's worth of new information arriving in a fortnight, with one pair of hands expected to catch all of it.

## Not disorganised, just buried

One mother put it plainly on Mumsnet: "I just cannot keep up with the constant requests for everything and only remember half of it." She wasn't fishing for sympathy. A few lines on she added, "I am organised, just overwhelmed by it." Eight messages some days from the school app, more things in the book bag, then back to her work inbox. None of it is difficult on its own. All of it landing on one person at once is a great deal to hold.

## A new term is mostly scheduling

Researchers at the Universities of Bath and Melbourne looked at how couples divide the household's mental load across two dozen everyday tasks. Mothers carried around 67 per cent more of them than fathers, and scheduling showed one of the widest gaps of all, ahead of cleaning and childcare (Weeks, Kowalewska and Ruppanner, Socius, 2025).

Most of it is logistics. New timetable, new club nights on new days, a changed pickup, homework starting up again. Every piece is a small when-and-who question, and they pile up with the same person each year.

## "We'll settle in soon"

There is a reassuring idea that the new routine beds in within a week or two and then runs itself. Habit research is less hopeful. In one University College London study, a new daily behaviour took about 66 days to feel automatic, and for plenty of people much longer. Until a routine reaches that point, someone is consciously running it every morning: that today is recorder day, that the kit needs washing tonight, that the club's booking site won't work until someone updates it for the new school year. One mother described doing exactly that, changing her daughter's year group on the club site and the violin teacher's site by hand, "for some reason we have to do this ourselves". Nobody sees that work. It only gets noticed when it doesn't happen.

## A fresh start you don't have to memorise

A reset should mean the whole family can see the new routine, not that one person commits it to memory and becomes the routine until it sticks. That is the part Youtine is being built for. The new timetable goes somewhere everyone can open, so the week isn't kept in a single memory. Put the club nights on a calendar the other parent actually checks and Wednesday swimming becomes something you both just know, instead of something you have to remember to say out loud.
